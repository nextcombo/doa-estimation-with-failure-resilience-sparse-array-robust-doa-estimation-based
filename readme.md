# DOA Estimation with Failure Resilience: Sparse Array Robust DOA Estimation Based on Deep Learning with Single-Snapshot [Document Code Included]

Introduction: A significant portion of real-world signals in the industrial domain contain patterns with cross-frequency trajectories, and some examples are provided below.
1) In induction motor current signals, there may be non-synchronous components of shaft speed, which may have intersection points on the time-frequency plane [1].
2) In vibration signals collected from rotating machinery, the synchronous frequency of shaft speed (e.g., harmonics and sidebands of gear meshing frequencies) may intersect with resonance frequencies [2].
3) Parts of vehicles include two or more power subsystems with unsynchronized speeds (e.g., hybrid vehicles, electric propulsion ships, and helicopters with electric tail rotors), and the vibration signals collected may contain frequency components synchronized with different power subsystems' rotational speeds, and these frequencies may have intersection points.
Frequency intersections can significantly affect the detection of fault-induced frequency patterns of interest in fault diagnosis. Therefore, developing a time-frequency method capable of handling such signals is crucial.
Linear transformations are a widely used class of time-frequency analysis methods. Linear transformations produce a set of bases to extract the amplitude and phase of signal patterns on the time-frequency plane. Short-time Fourier transform (STFT) employs a series of frequency-invariant basis functions, suitable for analyzing stationary or quasi-stationary signals [5]. Chirplet transforms [6]-[8] can be considered as an improved version of STFT. Their bases are set to a set of linear chirps, thus they have better capability to reveal signals containing time-varying frequencies. Post-processing techniques for time-frequency representations (TFR) can enhance the readability of linear transformations, such as synchronous compression transforms and their variants [9]-[11]. Their working principle is to remap the original TFR energy to new time-frequency positions based on the phase of the original TFR. However, neither linear transformations nor post-processing techniques can effectively handle non-stationary signals with cross-frequency components. Because in the time-frequency plane, the intersection of frequency trajectories requires two different modulation ratios for the two intersecting modes to detect them separately. However, linear transformations and post-processing techniques can only provide a foundation for one time-frequency point. Moreover, these methods assume that each time-frequency window covers only one signal mode. However, time-frequency windows can cover two crossing frequency points near the intersection. Due to interference between cross-frequency components, these methods suffer from trailing issues near the intersection points.
Adaptive mode decomposition methods are another class of time-frequency analysis methods [16], [17], including variational mode decomposition (VMD) [18]. Their working principle is to decompose the signal into several time modes, then calculate their instantaneous amplitude and frequency by demodulation. However, these methods require that each signal mode's energy concentrate within a very narrow bandwidth, and the linear modulation ratio is small. Additionally, in the mode detection step, modes may switch between different intersecting signal modes, leading to significant errors in subsequent demodulation due to mode mixing.
Traditional linear frequency modulation (LFM) decomposition methods [12] and [13] can decompose signals into a set of LFM signals, and generate TFR by summing the Wigner-Ville distributions of these LFM signals. They first generate a set of linear chirps in atomic form. Then, they iteratively calculate the residual signal (the original signal in the first iteration), and the inner product between atoms. The maximum inner product value at each iteration is taken as the weight for the corresponding atom. In this way, they decompose the signal into several linear LFM pulses. However, these methods have large errors for signals containing nonlinear LFM pulses.
The recently proposed adaptive linear frequency modulation (ACMD) decomposition [14] and variational nonlinear frequency modulation (VNCMD) decomposition [15] can be considered as a filter bank method. They use a time-frequency filter bank with a center frequency that changes over time to filter out the desired modes, and then obtain the frequencies of the modes through demodulation techniques. The initial center frequency is usually determined by short-time Fourier transform, so the accuracy of these methods largely depends on short-time Fourier transform. Short-time Fourier transform has blurring effects and sensitivity to noise when analyzing non-stationary signals, so its initial frequency may be significantly different from the true signal frequency. In this case, the extracted modes may be combinations of several true signal modes, resulting in significant error in the demodulation results.
As mentioned above, current time-frequency methods cannot well handle signals with cross-frequency trajectories. The key to solving this problem lies in the following points:
1) Providing two bases for two cross-frequency at the intersection;
2) Avoiding or eliminating mutual interference between two cross-frequencies in estimating their characteristics;
3) Avoiding frequency estimation errors caused by mode mixing.
Based on the above analysis, this paper proposes Adaptive Linear Frequency Modulation Wavelet Transform (ALCT). ALCT first computes the Linear Frequency Modulation Wavelet Transform (LCT) of signals with a series of linear frequency modulation ratios as parameters. Subsequently, ALCT iteratively estimates the features of the signal modes with the highest amplitude based on LCT and removes the energy of detected modes in the time-frequency modulation ratio plane. The iterational energy removal can avoid the interference between mode interruptions affecting the estimation of signal mode features. The signal features are directly estimated on the time-frequency plane without using demodulation techniques. Thus, even if the detected modes are mixed in different iterations, the energy position on the time-frequency plane remains unchanged. Although ALCT is an iterative method, it is also effective because the proposed algorithm can effectively obtain the LCT of the detected modes by directly weighting and shifting the LCT reference.
The rest of this paper is structured as follows: Section 2 introduces ALCT. Section 3 evaluates the proposed ALCT method using two synthetic signals. Section 4 further evaluates ALCT using real-world signals. Finally, Section 5 summarizes the paper.

Conclusion - A time-frequency analysis method, ALCT, is proposed for signals containing crossover points. It works by iteratively estimating the features of signal modes and directly separating the energy of detected modes on the time-frequency modulation plane. The advantages include the following aspects. 1) It can well reveal the vibration modes with frequency trajectory crossovers. 2) Since it does not need to calculate complex integral transforms in iterations, it is effective. 3) There is no frequency estimation error caused by mode mixing. The effectiveness of ALCT has been evaluated using two synthetic signals and three real-world signals. The limitation of ALCT is that there is still a slight error in estimating the amplitude of signal modes when two modes intersect near each other in time.
